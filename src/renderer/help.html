The idea in one minute

Nobody at a bench works on one thing from start to finish with a stopwatch running. You solder a ring, pickle it, set a stone in something else while it soaks, answer the phone, and go back. BenchClock doesn't ask you to time each of those. It asks two things:

  1. When did you sit down and when did you get up? That is one session: clock in, clock out.
  2. Roughly what share of that session went to each piece? You answer in percentages when you clock out.

From those two answers it keeps a running total for every piece, works out the time per piece when you make several, and can report on any week, month or stretch of days you like.

A three hour session, clocked out as 50% moonstone ring, 30% hoop earrings, the rest unassigned, puts 1h 30m on the ring, 54m on the hoops, and the remaining 36m on TimeOverhead.

Your first day, step by step

  1. Press Add a piece, type a name, tap what it is (ring, earrings, pendant…), press Add. Do that for each thing on your bench. It appears under On the bench, in the Getting started group.
  2. When you sit down to work, press the big Clock in button. The timer starts.
  3. Work. You can close the window or shut the computer down; you stay clocked in.
  4. When you stop, press Clock out. Type a percentage beside each piece you worked on and press Clock out again.
  5. When a piece is done, press Finish on its line.
  6. At the end of the week or the month, press Report or export for a PDF of where the time went.

Clocking in and out

The panel at the top shows whether you are clocked in, a running timer, and when the session began.

  • Clock in starts a session. Only one session runs at a time.
  • Closing the window does not clock you out. The session is written to disk the moment it starts, so it survives closing the app, restarting the computer, even a power cut. Open BenchClock again and the timer is still counting from when you clocked in.
  • Clock out opens the clock-out box, described in the next section. Keep working closes it without clocking out.
  • Discard session appears while you are clocked in. It throws the session away without logging any time. Use it if you clocked in by mistake.

If you forgot to clock out

Press Clock out as usual, then change the Clock-out time at the top of the box to when you really stopped. The session length and every piece's minutes are worked out again as you change it. The time has to be after the time you clocked in.

Dividing a session between pieces

The clock-out box lists every piece on the bench, plus anything you finished during this session. Beside each is a box for a percentage, and beside that, what the percentage comes to in hours and minutes.

  • Type a number next to the pieces you worked on. Leave the others blank.
  • The numbers don't have to reach 100. Whatever you don't hand out goes to TimeOverhead, and the line at the bottom shows the sum: Pieces 80% + TimeOverhead 20% = 100%.
  • They can't go over 100. If they do, the line turns red, tells you by how much, and Clock out waits until it is fixed.
  • Decimals are fine: 12.5 is accepted.

Two buttons that do the arithmetic

  • Split evenly shares the whole 100% across every piece listed. An old-style entry that stands for six pieces gets six shares.
  • Spread the rest leaves the numbers you typed alone and shares what is left among the boxes that are still blank. Type the ones you are sure of, then press it.

Groups in the clock-out box

Several of one design show as a single line with one box. A number typed there is divided evenly among them. Press the › mark to open the group and give each piece its own number; the group's box then shows what they add up to.

Percentages are estimates, and that is fine. "About half on the ring" recorded every day is worth far more than exact timings you stop keeping after a week.

TimeOverhead

TimeOverhead is the line that is always there, below the bench. It collects every part of a session you didn't give to a piece: ordering metal, photographing, packing, cleaning up, email, a customer at the door.

You never type a number for it. It simply receives what is left. It can't be finished, renamed or deleted, and its Log button shows every session that contributed to it.

It matters for pricing. The report shows what share of your clocked time is making and what share is TimeOverhead, which tells you how much the unpaid hours add to the true cost of a piece. BenchPrice uses that share to load your hourly rate.

Adding and editing pieces

Add a piece asks for:

  • Name of the piece or design. The only thing that is required.
  • What is it? Earrings, ring, pendant, chain, bracelet, cuff or bangle, brooch, custom, or other. This picks the icon and is shown in reports.
  • Photo, optional. See Photos.
  • How many? See Making several of one design.
  • SKU and notes, tucked under their own heading. Both appear on the piece's line, in reports and in the CSV.

A new piece starts under Getting started. The first time it receives time at clock-out it moves up to In progress, and the date is kept as the day it was started.

Editing and deleting

Edit on a piece's line changes any of the above. Delete piece is at the bottom left of that box. Deleting removes the piece and all the time logged on it, and can't be undone, so BenchClock asks first. If the piece is simply done, finish it instead.

Making several of one design

Set How many when you add the piece. Three moonstone rings are kept as three separate pieces that travel together as one line: Moonstone ring ×3, with a › mark in front.

  • Closed, the line works as a batch. It shows the total time and the average per piece, one number at clock-out is divided among them, and Finish all, Finish some and Edit all act on the whole group.
  • Open (press the name or the ›), each piece is listed as (1 of 3), (2 of 3)… with its own time, log, tick box, Finish and Edit. Use this when one of them took longer, or one is finished early.
  • Finish some asks how many are done and finishes the ones with the most time on them. To choose exactly which, open the group and tick them.
  • The same design added again later is a new group of its own. Rings made in March are never mixed in with the rings you add in June, so you can compare the two runs.

Photos

A photo on a piece is shown in place of its icon, on the lists, at clock-out and in the PDF report.

  • In the add or edit box, press Add… under Photo and choose a picture, or drop a picture anywhere on the box.
  • JPEG and PNG pictures work. iPhone HEIC photos need to be saved as JPEG first.
  • The picture is cropped to a square from its middle and shrunk. It is an icon, not an archive copy, so keep your originals.
  • Every photo you add stays in your library and is offered again for later pieces, which is handy for a design you make repeatedly. None goes back to the icon.
  • To remove a photo from the library, point at it and press the ×. Pieces that used it go back to their icon. BenchClock tells you how many before it removes anything.

Finishing and reopening

  • Finish on a line finishes that piece. On a group, Finish all or Finish some.
  • To finish several different pieces at once, tick them and press Mark finished at the top of the bench.
  • Finished pieces move to the Finished list at the bottom. Press Show to see it. The date they were finished is kept.
  • A piece finished in the middle of a session is still offered at that session's clock-out, so its last stretch of time isn't lost.
  • Reopen on a finished piece puts it back on the bench with all its time. Nothing is lost by finishing too early.

Tick boxes

Every piece, on the bench or finished, has a tick box. Ticks do two jobs:

  • Mark finished finishes the ticked pieces that are on the bench.
  • Report or export can be limited to the ticked pieces. See below.

Ticking a closed group ticks everything in it. The count of ticked pieces is shown at the top of the bench, next to Untick all. Ticks are not saved: they are gone when the app is closed.

The time log of a piece

Log on any line lists every session that put time on that piece: when you clocked in and out, the share of the session it was given, and what that came to. Time put on by hand is listed as an adjustment, with its day and reason (see the next section). The title shows the total. At the bottom is the name of the file the piece is stored in, in case you ever want to look at it directly. Adjust time… in this box opens the adjustment box for the piece.

Adjusting time by hand

Sessions are the normal way time gets onto a piece, but sometimes the clock wasn't running: work done before you had BenchClock, a morning you forgot to clock in, a share that came out wrong at clock-out. Adjust time puts time on a piece, or takes it off, without a session.

Open it from Edit › Adjust time… (Ctrl + T), which starts with whatever is ticked on the lists; or from Adjust time… in a piece's Log or Edit box, which starts with that piece. The box asks:

  • Which pieces? Tick any number, on the bench or finished. TimeOverhead is at the bottom of the list, for the day of ordering and packing you never clocked.
  • How much? Add or Take off, then hours and minutes. With more than one piece ticked, choose whether the amount goes to each piece or is shared between them evenly.
  • Day the work was done. Reports count the time on this day, so a week's report comes out right even when you catch up later. It starts as today.
  • Reason, optional. It is kept in the log so that in a month you know why the number changed.

The line above the buttons says what will happen before you press Adjust: Moonstone ring: 1h 20m → 1h 50m. A piece's time can't go below nothing, so taking off more than it has waits until the number is fixed.

  • An adjustment moves a piece from Getting started to In progress just as a session would.
  • Adjustments are not sessions. The number of sessions in a report and in the CSV counts real clock-ins only.
  • There is no undo, but an adjustment is undone by making the opposite one: took 30m off by mistake, add 30m back.
Moving time from one piece to another. You gave 40% of yesterday's session to the hoops and it should have been the pendant. Open the hoops' Log, read what 40% came to, say 48m, and take 48m off, dated yesterday. Then add 48m to the pendant, dated yesterday. The day's total is unchanged and both logs say why.

Reports and exports

Report or export, at the top right and in the File menu, opens one box that answers three questions.

1. Which pieces?

ChoiceWhat you get
EverythingEvery piece, and TimeOverhead. The only choice that can show how your clocked time divides between making and everything else.
TickedOnly the pieces you ticked on the lists before opening the box: one customer's commission, one collection, the pieces for a show. If anything is ticked when you open the box, this is chosen for you.
On the benchPieces that are not finished.
FinishedPieces that are finished.

2. Which days?

Press This week, Last week, This month, Last month, This year or All time, or set From and To yourself. Click the little calendar in a date box to pick a day from a calendar. Both days are included. Leave From empty for "everything up to", or To empty for "everything since".

  • Only time from those days is counted. A session counts on the day it was clocked in, even if it ran past midnight.
  • Pieces with nothing in those days are left out: a piece is included if it had time on those days, or was finished on them. Ticked pieces are the exception. You chose them by hand, so they are always listed, with 0h if need be.
  • The line under the dates tells you what your choices come to before you save anything: how many pieces, how much time, how many sessions.
  • Weeks start on the day your computer's region says they do, Sunday or Monday.

Your choice of days is remembered as "last week" rather than as dates, so the next time you open the box it means the week that is last week by then. That makes a Monday-morning report of last week two clicks.

3. PDF or CSV?

  • Save as PDF makes a report laid out for reading and printing, and opens it straight away. It starts with a summary, then lists pieces that were worked on and pieces that were finished, with photo or icon, status, date, number of sessions and time. Groups are totalled with each piece listed beneath. For a range of days there is a To date column as well: the time in those days, and beside it everything the piece has taken so far.
  • Save as CSV makes a file for a spreadsheet, one row per piece, with hours, minutes, minutes per piece and the number of sessions. For a range of days those figures count those days only. With Everything the last row is TimeOverhead.
Some reports worth making. Last week, everything: did the hours go where you meant them to? Last month, finished: what left the bench, and how long each piece really took (the To date column). All time, ticked: the full history of one commission, to check your quote against.

Calculators

Calculators at the top of the window, or Tools › Calculators (Ctrl + K), opens a window of bench arithmetic that can sit beside the app. Pick a calculator from the list on the left. Everything recalculates as you type, and what you typed is there again next time. Lengths are in millimetres, with inches alongside; weights are in grams.

They are guides, not gospel. Makers' ring charts differ by up to a quarter size, alloys vary by supplier, and metal is lost to the saw and the crucible. Cut long and file to fit; melt a little more than you need.

Ring blank

The length of stock to cut for a ring of a given size.

  • Asks: the size system (US, UK letters, EU, Japan, or an inside diameter in mm), the size, the metal's thickness, the band's width, whether to add room for the knuckle, and the allowance for filing and the joint (1.5 mm to start).
  • How it works: bent metal keeps its length along the middle of its thickness, so the stock needed is π × (inside diameter + thickness), plus the allowance so the ends can be filed true for a tight solder joint. A band over 6 mm wide has to pass the knuckle, so ticking the box adds 0.4 mm to the diameter, about half a size.
  • Gives: the cut length, the length along the middle, the inside diameter and circumference, and the same size in every other system. The chart underneath lists US 1 to 16 in half sizes against diameter, circumference, UK, EU and Japanese sizes, with your size marked.
  • Example: a US 7 in 1.5 mm stock is 17.28 mm inside; π × 18.78 = 58.99 mm, plus 1.5, so cut 60.5 mm.

Bangle or hoop

The same arithmetic for anything round made from stock: a bangle, a hoop earring, a large jump ring.

  • Asks: the inside diameter, the stock's thickness (the wire's diameter or the sheet's thickness), and the allowance.
  • Gives: the cut length, the inside circumference and the outside diameter. Bangles are usually 60 mm (small), 65 mm (medium) or 70 mm (large) inside.
  • Example: a 65 mm bangle in 2 mm wire: π × 67 = 210.5 mm, plus 1.5, so cut 212 mm.

Bezel strip

How long a strip of bezel to cut for a stone.

  • Asks: the stone's shape and measurements: diameter for a round stone, length and width for an oval or a rectangle, or the measured perimeter of a freeform stone (wrap a strip of paper or thread around it and measure that). Then the bezel's thickness and the overlap you like for the joint.
  • How it works: the stone's perimeter (an oval's by Ramanujan's approximation, which is good to a hair), plus π × the bezel's thickness because the outside of the bend travels farther than the inside, plus the overlap.
  • Gives: the cut length, and the perimeter with and without the bend. For a rectangle the corners are bent, not counted; a bezel that turns sharp corners is usually made in pieces and soldered.
  • Example: a 10 mm round cabochon in 0.3 mm bezel: 31.42 + 0.94 + 1 = 33.4 mm.

Jump rings

How much wire to wind for a batch of jump rings.

  • Asks: the mandrel's diameter (the inside of the ring), the wire gauge (or its diameter in mm), how many rings, and a waste allowance for the saw cut and mistakes.
  • How it works: each ring takes π × (mandrel + wire diameter) of wire, measured along the middle of the wire. Rings spring back a little after cutting, so the inside ends up 0.1 to 0.2 mm larger than the mandrel.
  • Gives: the total wire in mm, inches and feet, wire per ring, inside and outside diameters, the aspect ratio (inside diameter divided by wire diameter; under about 2.8 a ring is too tight to link with another of its own size, and chain maille weaves each want their own), and rings per inch of coil.
  • Example: 50 rings of 18 gauge (1.024 mm) on a 4 mm mandrel with 10% waste: 15.78 mm each, 868 mm in all, about 34 inches.

Metal weight and cost

What a piece of stock weighs, and costs, in any metal.

  • Asks: the metal (twenty, from fine silver to platinum, brass and titanium), the kind of stock (sheet, round, half-round, square or rectangular wire, tube, disc, or a ring band) and its measurements, a price per gram if you want the cost, and another metal to compare with.
  • How it works: the stock's volume times the metal's density. Densities are the usual figures; alloys vary a little by maker.
  • Gives: the weight in grams, pennyweight and troy ounces, the cost, the volume and density, and what the same piece would weigh in the other metal (in proportion to the densities: a ring that is 10 g in sterling is 15 g in 18k).
  • Example: a 50 × 20 × 1 mm sheet of sterling is 1000 mm³, which is 10.36 g.

Raise or lower purity

You have some metal and want it purer or less pure: sterling to 980, coin silver to sterling, 14k to 18k, 18k down to 14k.

  • Asks: what you have (fine silver, Britannia, sterling, coin silver, continental silver, 24k to 9k gold, or a purity you type), how much of it, and what you want. Purities can be given in fineness (of 1000), karat (of 24) or percent; picking from the list switches the unit to match.
  • How it works: going up, fine metal is added; going down, alloy is added. For silver the alloy is copper. For gold going down in karat, the alloy is silver and copper in a split you choose: Kent Raible blend (19 silver to 6 copper in 18k), yellow, pale yellow, rose, red, green, or your own, entered as percentages of the finished metal. Only the ratio of the two matters, so they needn't add to exactly the alloy share.
  • Gives: how much to add (fine silver and copper separately for gold), what you have once it is all melted together, the fine metal in it, and for gold what the finished metal is made of. Where you started from something that already had alloy in it, such as 22k, that part is named as "whatever alloy was in the 22k", since its make-up isn't known.
  • Examples: 100 g of sterling to 980: add 275 g of fine silver, giving 375 g. 50 g of coin silver to sterling: add 16.67 g of fine silver, giving 66.67 g. 75 g of fine gold to 18k Kent Raible blend: add 19 g of fine silver and 6 g of copper, giving 100 g.
  • Nothing added to sterling makes it pure: that takes refining, and the calculator says so. The What purity is it? box underneath turns fine metal and total weight into karat, fineness and percent.

Gold and silver alloys

Melt an alloy from fine metals, or melt two lots of different purity together.

  • Melt an alloy from fine metals asks for a recipe and the weight to make, and gives the grams of fine gold, fine silver and copper to melt. The recipes are 22k yellow; 18k yellow, Kent Raible blend, rose, red and green; 14k yellow, rose and red; 10k and 9k yellow; sterling 925, Britannia 958 and continental 800; and the Japanese shibuichi and shakudo. Change any percentage and the recipe becomes your own; the percentages needn't add to 100, the weight is shared in proportion. White golds need nickel or palladium master alloy and aren't here.
  • Melt two lots together asks for two weights and their purities (karat, fineness or percent) and gives what the melt comes out as and how much fine metal is in it. Type a purity you want and it says how much of lot B to add to lot A to reach it, or tells you when it can't be reached because the target isn't between the two.
  • Examples: 30 g of 18k yellow is 22.5 g of gold, 3.75 of silver and 3.75 of copper. 10 g of 18k and 10 g of 14k melt to 20 g of 16k.

Gold and silver recipe

The same recipes worked from what you have.

  • Asks: a recipe, and how many grams of one ingredient you have: fine gold, fine silver or copper.
  • How it works: the recipe says what share that ingredient is of the whole, so the whole follows, and so do the other ingredients.
  • Gives: the other ingredients to add, the weight of the melt, and what it is.
  • Example: 12 g of fine gold in the 18k Kent Raible blend calls for 3.04 g of fine silver and 0.96 g of copper, making 16 g of 18k.

Gauge to mm chart

American Wire Gauge, also called Brown & Sharpe, is what jewellery suppliers in North America use for wire and sheet alike. A higher number is thinner; each step is about 11% thinner, and six steps halves the thickness.

  • Asks: a gauge to turn into millimetres, and a measurement in millimetres to find the nearest gauge for.
  • Gives: both, and a chart of every gauge from 0 to 36 in millimetres and inches with what each is typically used for, your gauge marked.
  • Example: 18 gauge is 1.024 mm; 0.8 mm is nearest to 20 gauge (0.812 mm).

Units

Millimetres, centimetres and inches; grams, pennyweight, troy ounces, ordinary ounces and carats. Type a number, pick from and to. Underneath are the figures worth knowing by heart: an inch is 25.4 mm, a troy ounce 31.1035 g or 20 pennyweight, a carat 0.2 g, and a kitchen-scale ounce is 28.35 g, only 0.911 troy ounces.

Where your data lives, and backing it up

Everything is kept as plain, readable files in one folder on your own computer. Nothing is sent anywhere, and there is no account. The folder is named at the bottom of the main window; click it, or use File › Open the data folder.

SystemFolder
Linux~/.local/share/BenchClock
Windows%APPDATA%\BenchClock
macOS~/Library/Application Support/BenchClock

Inside are items (one file per piece), sessions (one file per clock-in and clock-out), photos (your photo library) and, while you are clocked in, current_session.json.

  • To back up, copy the whole folder somewhere safe. To restore or move to a new computer, copy it back to the same place.
  • To keep it somewhere else, such as a synced folder, set the environment variable BENCHCLOCK_DATA_DIR to the folder you want before starting BenchClock. If files change on disk while the app is open, it picks the changes up when you come back to its window.
  • Uninstalling or updating BenchClock leaves this folder alone.

BenchPrice: putting a price on the pieces

BenchClock keeps time; it doesn't do prices. BenchPrice is a separate, free app from the same place that does. Install it alongside BenchClock and it reads your time card: every finished piece with its making time, and the TimeOverhead share. For each piece you enter the metal and its weight in grams, and the stones, findings and anything else bought in at what you paid. It works out a price three ways (cost-plus, an hourly rate loaded with your TimeOverhead, and materials marked up by cost band), lets you set a price of your own instead, and writes the price you confirm into the piece's file.

  • Pieces made together (Moonstone ring ×3) are one set there and carry one price.
  • BenchPrice keeps its files in a pricing folder inside BenchClock's data folder and never changes BenchClock's own files, so backing up the folder backs up both.
  • Finish a piece here, then press F5 in BenchPrice to see it.

Download it from github.com/AlanEbell/benchprice.

Questions and fixes

I clocked in by mistake.

Press Discard session next to the clock. No time is logged.

I forgot to clock out last night.

Clock out now, and set Clock-out time in the box to when you really stopped.

I forgot to clock in.

A session can't be started in the past. Clock in now for the rest of the day, and put the missed time on the pieces with Edit › Adjust time, dated today. See Adjusting time by hand.

I gave the time to the wrong piece.

A clocked-out session can't be edited, but its result can be corrected: take the time off one piece and add it to the other with Adjust time, dated the day of the session. There is a worked example in Adjusting time by hand.

I worked on this before I had BenchClock.

Add the piece, then Adjust time to put the hours already spent on it, dated whenever the work was done.

I finished a piece too soon.

Show the Finished list and press Reopen on it.

My report is empty, or a piece is missing from it.

Look at Which days. A piece is listed only if it had time on those days or was finished on them. Press All time to see everything. Also check Which pieces: Ticked shows only what you ticked.

My photo won't import.

It is probably a HEIC picture from an iPhone. Save or export it as JPEG first.

I opened BenchClock twice.

Only one copy runs. Starting it again brings the window that is already open to the front.

Which version do I have?

Help › About BenchClock.
